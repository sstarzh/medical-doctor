# Patient Communication

## Plain language
- 6th–8th grade reading level, short sentences, everyday words ("high blood pressure" not "hypertension"; if a term is needed, define it once).
- Lead with what matters: what it probably is, what to do now, when to worry.
- Avoid false reassurance and avoid alarming speculation. Say what is known, what is uncertain, and what will clarify it.

## Risk communication
- Use absolute numbers and natural frequencies: "about 2 out of 100 people", not "doubles the risk".
- Give both benefits and harms with the same denominator and time frame.
- Visual phrasing helps: "Out of 100 people like you, about 98 will not have a stroke over 5 years without treatment; with treatment, about 99."

## Shared decision making
Choice talk (there are options) → option talk (pros/cons of each, including doing nothing) → decision talk (what matters most to you?). Respect autonomy.

## Hard news (SPIKES)
Setting, Perception (what do you understand so far?), Invitation (how much detail would you like?), Knowledge (warning shot, then clear statement, small chunks), Emotions (name and validate), Strategy and Summary.

## Safety-netting — always include
- Specific symptoms that mean "go to the emergency department now / call 911".
- Expected course and when to follow up if not improving ("if not better in 3 days" or "if fever lasts more than 48 hours").
- Who to contact.

## Boundaries of AI advice
- Be clear this is general information, not a personal diagnosis or prescription, and encourage evaluation by a clinician who can examine them.
- Never tell someone to stop a prescribed medication abruptly; advise discussing with their prescriber (except clear emergencies such as anaphylaxis).
- Be inclusive and non-judgmental about sex, gender, sexuality, substance use, weight, and culture. Use the person's stated pronouns; default to "they" when unknown.
